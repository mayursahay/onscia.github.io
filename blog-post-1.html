The art of returning to stillness

October 2026
5 min read
By Lipi

We live in an age of constant motion. Our attention is pulled outward by notifications, demands, and endless mental chatter. There are moments when the world around us is quiet, yet the mind continues to move. Thoughts revisit the past, anticipate the future, replay conversations and imagine possibilities. Even in the absence of immediate demands, the mind can remain occupied with what has happened, what might happen and what we wish were different.

We often associate stillness with the absence of activity, ranging from sitting in a quiet room overlooking a peaceful landscape to at least a few moments away from our daily responsibilities. But is stillness simply the absence of external noise, or is it something we can cultivate within ourselves, even as life continues to unfold?

In the Yoga Sutras, Patanjali describes the essence of yoga as the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind.

Yoga Sutra 1.2

This short sutra offers a perspective on yoga that extends far beyond physical postures or flexibility. Patanjali directs our attention towards chitta, the mind in its broader sense, including the movements of thought, perception, memory and mental impression.

The word vritti refers to the movements or modifications of the mind. These may take the form of thoughts, memories, interpretations, expectations, desires or reactions. They are a natural part of being human. Our minds continuously process experiences, make connections and respond to the world around us.

Yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ - Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind

The word nirodha is often translated as cessation, restraint or stilling. It does not necessarily mean forcibly suppressing thoughts or attempting to make the mind blank. Rather, it points towards the settling of mental fluctuations and a change in how we relate to them.

A thought may arise, but we do not have to follow it endlessly. An emotion may emerge, but we do not have to let it determine every response. An experience may be unpleasant, but it need not define our entire inner state. Patanjali expands on this in the following sutra

Yoga Sutra 1.3

When the fluctuations of the mind are stilled, the seer (drashta) rests in its own nature.

Tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam - Then the seer abides in its own true nature

We are accustomed to identifying closely with our thoughts, emotions, roles and experiences. A moment of anxiety can make the future seem uncertain; a moment of success can shape how we perceive ourselves. Yet these experiences change. The possibility of observing them, rather than being entirely absorbed by them, opens a different way of relating to life. Stillness, in this sense, is not an escape from thought or emotion. It is the cultivation of awareness that allows us to observe the mind without being continually carried away by its movements.

Stillness is not the absence of movement

It is easy to imagine stillness as a state in which nothing disturbs us, everything is under control and life unfolds peacefully. But life is inherently changing. Circumstances shift, relationships evolve, responsibilities grow and unexpected experiences arise.

We may not always be able to choose what happens to us, but we can gradually develop greater awareness of how we respond. We can learn to recognise a reaction before acting on it, to observe a desire without immediately following it, and to pause before allowing an emotion to shape our decisions.

A note on practice

This is where yoga becomes relevant to the ordinary rhythms of contemporary life. Its practice need not be confined to a mat or a meditation cushion. The awareness cultivated during practice can extend into the way we work, communicate, make decisions and relate to the people around us.

The purpose is not to become unaffected by life, but to meet it with greater clarity.
